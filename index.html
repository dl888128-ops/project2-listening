MEANING FIRST, THEN MAKING RECOMMENDATIONS

Activity A · Contextual Meaning

Mini lesson · Describing effects

Detrimental and beneficial are adjectives: they describe effects. Detrimental means causing harm or negative effects; beneficial means producing positive or helpful effects.

Patterns: be detrimental to + noun · be beneficial to + noun. Change be to fit the subject: is, are, or can be. After to, name who or what is affected.

PBS transcript excerpt — teenager: I think it's 100% detrimental to me. This describes her experience, not every teenager’s experience.

Instructional examples — not PBS quotations:
Late-night scrolling can be detrimental to sleep.
A supportive group chat can be beneficial to friendships.

Activity B · Giving Advice and Recommendations

Mini lesson · Turn a concern into advice

Advice recommends an action; add a reason so your listener can decide whether it fits their situation. Choose a respectful tone when advising a classmate.

PBS transcript excerpts: Dr. Faheem begins My advice for parents would be. In the later advice-to-parents passage, another speaker says Listen to your children. The second excerpt is an imperative: it begins with a base verb and recommends an action. These short excerpts retain the transcript wording.

Patterns and instructional examples — not PBS quotations:

  • My advice would be to + base verb introduces a recommendation gently: My advice would be to silence alerts during homework.
  • You should + base verb gives a clear recommendation: You should put your phone out of reach while studying.
  • Consider + -ing offers an option for the listener to weigh: Consider taking a screen break before bed. Use taking, not to take, after consider.
  • Imperative: base verb gives a direct action: Turn off nonessential alerts during homework. A friendly context or please can soften the tone.

Connect advice to a reason with because or so. These classroom patterns help you write your own advice; they are not all claimed as expressions used in the recording.

06 · Your Turn — Final Recommendation

COMMUNICATIVE OUTPUT TASK

Situation: Your school student council is choosing a priority for a digital well-being campaign. As a Grade 11 student adviser, write a message for fellow Grade 11 students. Your purpose is to recommend responsible social media practices that support teenagers’ digital well-being.

Your deliverable: Write an 80–120-word digital well-being campaign message in English.

  1. Refer to at least one specific idea from the PBS report. Identify it as the report’s idea; keep your own advice separate.
  2. Give two practical recommendations for fellow Grade 11 students, with a reason for each.
  3. Decide which recommendation your school should prioritize. Explain why that action matters most or is the most realistic first step for your classmates.
  4. Use an advice pattern from Activity B: My advice would be to…, You should…, Consider + -ing, or an appropriate imperative. Try to use detrimental to or beneficial to to explain an effect.

Plan briefly: Which report idea supports your message? What two actions fit school life? Which should come first, and why? Use your own words; clear, short sentences are enough.

Self-assessment after submission






Illustrative model response — teaching example

This original example is not a quotation from PBS or an evaluation of your writing. Its recommendations are the example writer’s suggestions. It illustrates two recommendations with reasons and a justified school priority. Make your own choices and use your own words.

Keep Connection, Protect Your Time

Grade 11 friends, let’s keep useful connections without losing study time. In the PBS report, Susan Salhaney explains that social media keeps people connected but can shape their view of reality. You should silence nonessential alerts during homework because fewer interruptions can help you concentrate. Consider checking online portrayals against life beyond the screen because a limited picture can distort expectations. Our school should prioritize silencing alerts: classmates can try it immediately without giving up supportive chats. Protecting study time can be beneficial to learning. These are our campaign suggestions, not recommendations quoted from PBS.

Improve your next draft

  • Evidence: Name the speaker or describe a specific report idea accurately. If you use the study, write “linked to” or “associated with,” not “proved to cause.”
  • Clarity: Give two actions a teenager can realistically take and connect each to a reason with “because” or “so.”
  • Language: Check your advice pattern and the noun after detrimental to or beneficial to, if used. Make sure each reason fits the action, and explain why your school should prioritize one recommendation. Read your message aloud and revise unclear sentences. Aim for 80–120 words.
